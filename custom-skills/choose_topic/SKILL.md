# Choose the Topic

## What a topic is
The history of or fact or feature something young men own (clothes, shoes, accessories) whose real reason is a genuine surprise, even to a guy who already watches clothing content.

## The reason jump (main test)
For each candidate, privately note what most people would guess the reason is, and the true reason.
- If the truth is just the guess with more detail, science or numbers, discard it.
- Keep it only if the truth comes from a different world than the guess. Guesses are almost always about comfort, function or style. Winning answers usually come from tax and tariff law, labeling law, manufacturing limits, shipping and retail logistics, liability, counterfeiting, cost-cutting, marketing psychology, or an accident of history that stuck.

## The informed viewer
Assume the viewer already knows popular clothing facts: the watch pocket, copper rivets, selvedge lines, the bomber jacket's orange lining, locker loops, how Gore-Tex breathes, polyester smelling worse than cotton, heel-lock eyelets, why clothes pill or shrink. Anything that appears in "clothing facts you didn't know" listicles is already known. Discard it.

## Where to search (changed 2026-11-06, Dario; rebalanced 2026-11-10, Dario)
Discovery first, verification second. Patents are good for proof, bad for finding topics, so do NOT search primary sources first. Do not start a topic search in patents, CBP rulings, court cases, or other law sources. Start with interesting facts, crazy facts, interesting stories, how it works, and how it was invented. Tariff and trademark topics are allowed only if they turn up that way, they are not the search target.
- Invention stories and clothing technologies come FIRST (Dario 2026-11-10). Prefer topics where something was invented — a new material, a machine, a process, a technology — with an inventor, a problem they were solving, and a mechanism you can show working. Dario 2026-11-10: the search was only finding features of clothing ("that detail was for X") and not invention stories or technologies, and those are boring. A static feature's original purpose is the weakest kind of topic and only survives if the mechanism is genuinely surprising and showable on the object.
- Search broadly for discovery: crazy facts, weird and surprising clothing facts, inventions, stories behind a feature, and really cool clothing science. Use listicles, forums, social posts, trade magazines, and company stories as discovery leads only, never as proof.
- Really cool clothing science is good too, as long as it passes the reason jump and the informed viewer would not already know it. Science that is just the guess with more detail still fails.
- Then verify the survivors. Primary sources are still the check, not the search: U.S. Customs (CBP) CROSS rulings, patents, FTC labeling rules, court cases and trade disputes, apparel trade and sourcing publications, supplier documentation, industry standards, company filings and earnings calls.
- For a story or invention, verify that the feature actually shipped, the thing was actually made or worn, and the story is told by at least two independent credible sources. If a story has only one source, or the sources disagree, discard it.

Clues that a hidden reason exists: a feature with no obvious use or that wears off fast; a feature on one country's, gender's or price tier's version of a product but not another's; an oddly specific blend, measurement or percentage; a sudden change in a specific year.

## Brand balance (Dario, 2026-11-11)
Single-brand product topics are allowed sometimes, but not every time. A topic counts as single-brand if it is a trademarked product or technology owned by one company, for example Nike Air, BOA, HEATTECH, Champion Reverse Weave. A topic counts as generic if it is a material, construction, mechanism, or science that many brands use, even if one brand is mentioned as an example.
- No more than 1 single-brand topic in any 3 delivered videos, and never 2 single-brand topics in a row.
- Before choosing, check the last delivered topics in the USED TOPICS list. If the last 2 delivered were both single-brand, the next topic must be generic. If the last 3 already include 1 single-brand topic, prefer a generic survivor unless the single-brand survivor is clearly stronger on proof, reason distance, and showability.
- A single-brand topic must earn its place: the invention must be exceptional, not just a brand technology, and the narration must focus on the inventor and the mechanism, never on brand praise or an ad tone.
- Do not ban single-brand topics outright, and do not force a weak generic topic just to hit the quota. If no generic survivor passes all gates, report that, do not lower the bar, and let Dario decide whether to use a single-brand topic anyway.

## Gates (a candidate must pass every one)
1. Most viewers own or have seen the object.
2. It passes the reason jump.
3. The informed viewer would not already know it.
4. Every step can be shown on the object in low-poly 3D: exploded view, cross-section, layers separating, parts moving. Anything that needs maps, charts, documents or people fails.
5. It is backed by two independent credible sources. At least one primary (ruling, patent, regulation, court record or company document) is required when the claim is about law, tariffs, labeling, or a patent. For a story, invention, or science claim, two independent credible sources that agree are enough, but listicles, content farms and social posts alone do not count as proof.
6. It is not on the used-topics list.

Note: Dario's original list numbered these gates 1 to 4, then 7 and 8. Renumbered here to 1 to 6 with no content change, to close the gap.

## How to decide
1. Search broadly and gather at least 20 candidates from crazy facts, inventions, stories, and cool clothing science.
2. Discard every candidate that fails any gate.
3. Choose among the survivors in this order of priority, moving to the next rule only to break a tie:
   a. Invention or technology story over a static feature-origin story (Dario 2026-11-10)
   b. Stronger proof (primary source beats secondary)
   c. Bigger distance between the guess and the truth
   d. Easier to show on a single object
   e. More relevant to what young men with style wear now
   f. Brand balance (Dario 2026-11-11): no more than 1 single-brand topic in any 3 delivered videos, never 2 in a row, prefer generic when the recent run is already brand-heavy
4. If nothing survives, search a different object and a different source type, and repeat. After three rounds with no survivor, stop and report that no verified topic was found. Never lower the bar and never make up a topic.

## Calibration
GOOD: Why many Converse have felt on the bottom. Guess: grip or comfort. Truth: covering at least half the sole with fabric lets the shoe be classified as a slipper, which carries a much lower import tariff. The felt wears off within weeks, a clue it was never for the wearer.
GOOD: Why some Columbia shirts have a pocket below the waist. Guess: storage. Truth: under U.S. tariff rules, certain women's garments with pockets below the waist pay lower duty. This is a women's garment rule, so it is a pattern example only.
GOOD (invention story): Nike Air. Guess: a cushion in the sole. Truth: a NASA rocket engineer, Marion Frank Rudy, invented a gas-filled polyurethane pouch with large-molecule gas that would not leak, pitched it to 23 companies, and Nike built it into the Tailwind. The video is about an invention, an inventor, and a mechanism you can show working.
REJECT: Where the zipper came from (origin trivia). Why jeans have a tiny pocket (listicle staple). Why polyester gym shirts smell worse (the guess, zoomed in). How Gore-Tex blocks rain but lets sweat out (the guess, zoomed in, widely known).
REJECT: A static feature's original purpose with no invention, no technology, and no mechanism to show — "that pocket was for tickets", "that V was for sweat", "those buttons were for polo". The truth is just the feature's first job, and there is nothing being invented or working on screen. Dario called this whole class boring, 2026-11-10.
Both good examples involve tariffs, but tariffs are not the pattern. The pattern is a visible feature whose real purpose lives somewhere the viewer never thinks about.

## Lock the topic
Before moving on, fix these for yourself:
- The object, and the exact part the camera opens on
- The guess and the truth
- The mechanism chain: 3 to 5 steps, one sentence each, using only verified facts
- What moves in the shot for each step
- The verified fact, in one sentence

## STAGE 2: CHOOSE THE HOOK
The hook must:
- Name the opening-shot part within the first few words
- Be about the viewer's own clothes or habits
- Create one question and not answer it
- Contain one surprise that is true according to the verified fact, and nothing more
- Be 12 words or fewer
- Not open with "Did you know," "You won't believe," "Here's why," "Nobody tells you" or anything similar
Vary the angles, and skip any angle the facts don't support. Never invent people, stories, dates or numbers.
Discard any hook that breaks a rule. From the rest, pick the one that names the part soonest and states the surprise most plainly. It becomes the narrator's first line.
