Verified fact: the air bubble in a Nike sole came from a NASA aerospace engineer, not a shoe designer. Frank Rudy used a semi-permeable membrane to trap a large-molecule gas inside a polyurethane pouch, so it compresses when you step and springs back, and he was turned down by 23 shoe companies before Nike said yes.

Guess: the bubble is just air, or foam, for comfort, or a marketing gimmick.
Truth: rocket engineering. A gas-filled membrane from an aerospace engineer, first tried in ski boots, first in a running shoe in 1978, and only made visible in 1987 when a designer cut a window in the sole after seeing an inside-out building in Paris.

Mechanism chain, verified facts only:
1. Marion Frank Rudy was an aerospace engineer who worked with NASA on Saturn and Apollo rocket engines, and learned blow rubber moulding there.
2. He made a polyurethane pouch, a semi-permeable membrane that traps a large-molecule gas inside, so it compresses under a step and springs back, instead of foam wearing out.
3. He first used it to enhance the fit and support of ski boots, then pivoted it to running shoes.
4. He pitched it to 23 other shoe companies first, all said no, then met Nike co-founder Phil Knight in March 1977, and Nike approved it after Knight tested a demo in a Cortez.
5. The first shoe was the Nike Air Tailwind, released at the Honolulu Marathon in December 1978, and its silver paint tore the upper apart within weeks, not the air sole. In 1987 the Air Max 1 cut a window in the midsole, inspired by the Centre Pompidou in Paris, an inside-out building, so you could finally see the air bag.

Sources:
- US Patent 4,183,156, inventor Marion F. Rudy, filed September 6, 1977, granted January 15, 1980, "Insole construction for articles of footwear", https://patents.google.com/patent/US4183156A/en (primary source; gas-filled chambers in a shoe sole)
- Nike, Department of Nike Archives, "Never Done Imagining: The Power of Air", https://www.nike.com/ca/a/never-done-imagining-power-of-air (primary company source; semi-permeable membrane trapping a large-molecule gas, first used in ski boots, 20-minute test run in a Cortez gained Knight's approval, Tailwind silver paint caused the shoe to fall apart within weeks)
- Nike, Department of Nike Archives, "The History of the Air Max 1", https://www.nike.com/jp/en/a/air-max-1-history (primary company source; air bag like a trampoline, Centre Pompidou inside-out building inspired Tinker Hatfield to cut away the midsole to expose the air bag)
- MR Porter, "Walking On Air: How Nike's Tailwind 79 Sneaker Sparked A Revolution", https://www.mrporter.com/en-au/journal/fashion/nike-tailwind-79-air-sneakers-history-1318476 (independent source; Rudy was a NASA aerospace engineer, pitched to 23 other shoe companies before Nike in 1977, Tailwind debuted at the 1978 Honolulu Marathon)
- Sneaker Freaker, "Nike's Nutty Professor: The Story of Air Inventor Frank Rudy", https://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/history-nike-air-sole-inventor-frank-rudy (independent source; blow rubber moulding, March 1977 meeting with Phil Knight, Tailwind 1978, silver upper tearing)

Caveats, do not smooth these away in narration:
- Do not claim the air sole is a piece of a spacesuit or an Apollo helmet transplanted into a shoe. Nike's own archive identifies Rudy as a former NASA engineer, and secondary sources link blow rubber moulding to Apollo helmets, but the safe narration is "a rocket engineer" and "the process he learned working on rockets", not a transplanted spacecraft part.
- Do not use a pair count for the first Tailwind run. Sources disagree: 230 pairs in one account, 250 in another. Say it sold out, with no number, or leave the sell-out out entirely.
- Do not use a six-mile test run. Nike's archive says a simple 20-minute test run in a Cortez. Use that, or say only that Knight tested it himself.
- The patent was filed in 1977 and granted in 1980, so do not say it was patented in 1977, and do not say Rudy patented it in 1979 without a source. The Gizmodo account says patented in 1979, the patent record says granted January 15, 1980. Use the patent record date if a date is needed.
- The gas is a large-molecule gas, not ordinary air. Do not call it "just air" in the narration, except to correct that guess in the hook.
