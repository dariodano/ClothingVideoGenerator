# Clothing Explained: Converse Chuck Taylor felt sole

Topic chosen by the Choose the Topic skill, prepared 2026-11-06, NOT built yet. No video made, no narration recorded, no image generated. Awaiting Dario's verdict on the blank red tab video before building.

Verified fact: the fuzzy felt on the bottom of a fresh Chuck Taylor is there for tariff classification, not for grip or comfort. With fabric covering more than half of the ground-contacting sole, the shoe can be classified in a lower-duty footwear category, like a slipper, instead of as a rubber-soled sneaker.

Guess: the fuzz is for grip, comfort, or quiet steps.
Truth: tariff engineering. The felt puts the shoe in a cheaper import category, and it wears off within weeks of walking.

Mechanism chain, verified facts only:
1. A fresh Chuck Taylor has felt covering the edges and arch of its rubber sole, leaving bare rubber windows in the middle and heel.
2. United States tariff classification turns on the material covering 50 percent or more of the bottom surface of the shoe that contacts the ground.
3. With fabric covering more than half that surface, the shoe can fall in a lower-duty category, like a slipper, instead of a rubber-soled sneaker.
4. The patent for this kind of sole states duty can range from 37.5 percent ad valorem for many common types of footwear to 3 percent ad valorem for certain types.
5. The felt wears off within weeks of walking, while the import saving was already taken at the border.

Sources:
- US Patent 6,571,491 B2, Safdeye, Wu and Stein, granted June 3, 2003, "Shoe having a fabric outsole", https://patents.google.com/patent/US6571491B2/en (primary source; lines in the patent text state the 50 percent rule and the 37.5 percent to 3 percent duty range, and that greater than 50 percent of the ground-contacting surface includes fabric material)
- Gear Patrol, March 9, 2024, "The Real Reason Converse Chuck Taylors Have Fuzzy Soles Might Surprise You", https://www.gearpatrol.com/footwear/converse-chuck-taylor-fuzzy-soles/ (applies the patent to Chuck Taylors, says soles over 50 percent felt classify as a slipper)
- Smithsonian Magazine, September 11, 2013, "This Is Why Your Converse Sneakers Have Felt on the Bottom", https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/this-is-why-your-converse-sneakers-have-felt-on-the-bottom-6016648/?no-ist (same mechanism, same 37.5 percent to 3 percent range)

Caveats, do not smooth these away in narration:
- The patent itself is assigned to ES Originals, not Converse, and its drawings look more like slippers than sneakers. Gear Patrol and Smithsonian are the sources tying this sole design to Chuck Taylors, so the narration must say "a patent for this kind of sole", not "Converse's patent".
- Smithsonian quotes the same 50 percent language but cites the patent as 6,471,491. The patent found and read is US 6,571,491 B2, and a later patent (US 7,845,096) also cites Safdeye 6,571,491, so 6,571,491 is the number to use. Do not use 6,471,491.
- Duty rates are not a single settled pair. The patent states 37.5 percent to 3 percent. Gear Patrol gives slippers as 3 percent to about 25 percent. Other tariff-engineering write-ups give different pairs for different years and classifications. Use only the patent's stated range, and do not claim Converse pays exactly 3 percent today.
- The skill's calibration example is this exact topic, so it was the expected survivor, but it still passed every gate on its own sources, it was not chosen just because it was the example.
